# The canon

The scout's raw material. Seeded August 27, 2026, and it grows: every run that finds a good paper
the file does not have adds it, whether or not that paper is the one written up.

**Every entry below was checked against OpenAlex on August 27, 2026.** Titles, years, authors and
venues are as that index reports them. Nothing here is from memory, because a fabricated citation
is the one failure this publication could not survive, and two of my own recollections were wrong
before the check corrected them.

**ACCESS is stated per entry and it is not a formality.** `FREE FULL TEXT` means the PDF was
actually fetched and returned. `NEEDS HUNT` means OpenAlex reports no open version and the ladder
in `SPEC.md` has not been run against it yet, so it may still be reachable or may be a dead end.
A `NEEDS HUNT` paper is a fine candidate; it just has to clear the gate before it costs a run.

## Agents, incentives, and how a sale actually gets made

**Levitt & Syverson (2005), "Market Distortions when Agents are Better Informed: The Value of
Information in Real Estate Transactions."** NBER working paper 11053.
ACCESS: **FREE FULL TEXT**, `https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w11053/w11053.pdf`
The trick: they could not ask agents whether they sell client houses too cheaply and too fast, so
they compared houses agents sold for clients against houses those same agents owned. The
commission math is the whole explanation, which is why this is a story about incentives rather
than about dishonesty. **This is the strongest first piece in the file.**

**Rutherford, Springer & Yavaş (2005), "Conflicts between principals and agents: evidence from
residential brokerage."** Journal of Financial Economics. 322 citations.
ACCESS: **NEEDS HUNT**. The independent test of the same question as Levitt and Syverson, which
makes it the natural "has it held up" companion rather than a second piece.

**Barwick & Pathak (2015), "The costs of free entry: an empirical study of real estate agents in
Greater Boston."** RAND Journal of Economics, doi `10.1111/1756-2171.12082`. 78 citations.
ACCESS: **NEEDS HUNT**. What happens to a market when commissions are fixed but entry is not.

**Hendel, Nevo & Ortalo-Magné (2009), "The Relative Performance of Real Estate Marketing Platforms:
MLS versus FSBOMadison.com."** 200 citations.
ACCESS: **NEEDS HUNT**. A rare natural experiment on what the MLS is actually worth to a seller.

**Northcraft & Neale (1987), "Experts, amateurs, and real estate: An anchoring-and-adjustment
perspective on property pricing decisions."** Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes.
1,091 citations.
ACCESS: **NEEDS HUNT**. They walked real estate agents through a real house and gave different
groups different list prices. The agents anchored, and said they had not. Directly about pricing a
listing, which is the `sales` section's core question.

## Sellers, buyers, and behavior

**Genesove & Mayer (2001), "Loss Aversion and Seller Behavior: Evidence from the Housing Market."**
NBER working paper 8143.
ACCESS: **FREE FULL TEXT**, `https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w8143/w8143.pdf`
Boston condo records let them see what each seller originally paid, so they could test whether
someone facing a nominal loss lists higher and waits longer. They do. Explains a behavior every
agent in Greenville has seen and nobody has been able to name.

**Case & Shiller (1988), "The Efficiency of the Market for Single-Family Homes."** NBER working
paper 2506.
ACCESS: **FREE FULL TEXT**, `https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w2506/w2506.pdf`
OpenAlex reports no open version for this one; the working paper is free anyway. A good worked
example of why step 3 of the access ladder exists.

## Value, place, and what a house is worth

**Rosen (1974), "Hedonic Prices and Implicit Markets: Product Differentiation in Pure
Competition."** Journal of Political Economy, doi `10.1086/260169`. **10,743 citations.**
ACCESS: **NEEDS HUNT**, and it may be a genuine dead end given its age.
The paper underneath every automated valuation and every price-per-square-foot argument ever made
in a listing appointment. If the full text cannot be reached, do not write it from summaries.

**Bayer, Ferreira & McMillan (2004), "Tiebout Sorting, Social Multipliers and the Demand for School
Quality."** NBER working paper 10871.
ACCESS: **FREE FULL TEXT**, `https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w10871/w10871.pdf`
How much of a school-zone price premium is the school, and how much is the neighbors. Fair housing
applies in full to anything written from this one.

**Aaronson, Faber, Hartley et al. (2020), "The long-run effects of the 1930s HOLC redlining maps on
place."** Regional Science and Urban Economics, doi `10.1016/j.regsciurbeco.2020.103622`. 169
citations.
ACCESS: **NEEDS HUNT**; a Chicago Fed working paper version is the likely route.
Pairs with the Greenville HOLC map itself (see the history sources below), which is what would make
it a Rebrew piece rather than a summary of somebody else's.

## Sales performance

**Verbeke, Dietz & Verwaal (2010), "Drivers of sales performance: a contemporary meta-analysis."**
Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, doi `10.1007/s11747-010-0211-8`. 621 citations.
ACCESS: **OPEN**, Springer reports an open PDF at
`https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11747-010-0211-8.pdf` (fetch it to confirm).
What actually predicts whether a salesperson performs, ranked. Note this is a meta-analysis, so the
one-paper rule holds but THE TRICK is the aggregation method itself, and the honest version has to
say what a meta-analysis can and cannot establish.

**Churchill, Ford, Hartley & Walker (1985), "The Determinants of Salesperson Performance: A
Meta-Analysis."** Journal of Marketing Research, doi `10.2307/3151357`. 1,196 citations.
ACCESS: **NEEDS HUNT**. The 1985 answer to the same question Verbeke asked in 2010. Reading them
against each other is a ready-made "has it held up" piece, and it is the one case where naming the
second paper is clearly correct.

# Urban economics: why land costs what it costs

**Added August 27, 2026 with the widening.** The file launched weighted toward residential brokerage
and behavioral economics, which is one slice of the discipline. These layers are the foundation, and
local coverage never touches them. All checked against OpenAlex the same day.

**Sáiz (2010), "The Geographic Determinants of Housing Supply."** Quarterly Journal of Economics,
doi `10.1162/qjec.2010.125.3.1253`. **2,020 citations.**
ACCESS: **NEEDS HUNT**, and a Wharton working paper version is the likely route.
He measured how much land a metro physically cannot build on, using satellite elevation and water
data, and showed that geography plus regulation explains why identical demand produces wildly
different prices in different cities. The single best answer to why one metro is expensive and
another is not.

**Glaeser & Gyourko (2002), "The Impact of Zoning on Housing Affordability."** NBER working paper
8835. (Published later as the building-restrictions work; OpenAlex lists a 2003 RePEc version too.)
ACCESS: **FREE FULL TEXT**, `https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w8835/w8835.pdf`
The trick: compare what a house sells for against what it costs to build one more. Where price sits
far above construction cost, the gap is a regulatory tax, and they price it. This is the mechanism
underneath every argument about housing supply in every city council meeting.

**Gyourko, Mayer & Sinai (2006), "Superstar Cities."** NBER working paper 12355; published in
American Economic Journal: Economic Policy (2013), doi `10.1257/pol.5.4.167`.
ACCESS: **FREE FULL TEXT**, `https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12355/w12355.pdf`
Why a handful of metros pull away permanently while the rest track income. Directly relevant to
whether Greenville's run is a cycle or a level shift, which is the question underneath most local
real estate argument.

**Mills (1967), "An Aggregative Model of Resource Allocation in a Metropolitan Area."** 1,145
citations.
ACCESS: **NEEDS HUNT**, and likely a dead end given its age.
One of the founding papers of the monocentric city model, which is still the mental furniture behind
every conversation about density, commutes, and land gradients.

# Development: what a developer is actually buying

**Titman (1985), "Urban Land Prices Under Uncertainty."** American Economic Review. 645 citations.
ACCESS: **NEEDS HUNT**.
Why it can be rational to leave a valuable lot empty. Building commits you to one use, so waiting has
option value, and uncertainty makes that option worth more. Explains vacant downtown parcels better
than any story about lazy owners, and it is a mechanism that transfers to every hold-versus-build
decision.

**Capozza & Helsley (1989), "The fundamentals of land prices and urban growth."** Journal of Urban
Economics, doi `10.1016/0094-1190(89)90003-x`. 486 citations.
ACCESS: **NEEDS HUNT**.
Decomposes raw land price into agricultural value, the cost of conversion, accessibility, and the
value of expected future growth. That decomposition IS the land residual a developer runs, written
formally.

# Tenants and income-producing assets

**Pashigian & Gould (1998), "Internalizing Externalities: The Pricing of Space in Shopping Malls."**
Journal of Law and Economics, doi `10.1086/467386`. 201 citations.
ACCESS: **NEEDS HUNT**.
Anchor tenants pay dramatically less rent per square foot than the small shops beside them, and it
is correct that they do: the anchor generates the traffic the small shops pay for. **The cleanest
answer in the literature to which businesses are good tenants and why**, and the model transfers
straight to any multi-tenant property.

**Wheaton (1987), "The Cyclic Behavior of the National Office Market."** Real Estate Economics, doi
`10.1111/1540-6229.00433`. 257 citations.
ACCESS: **NEEDS HUNT**.
Why office overbuilds on a long, repeating cycle. Construction lags demand by years, so everyone
responds to the same signal and delivers into the same glut. The mechanism generalizes to industrial
and multifamily, and it is the reason "the fundamentals are strong" is a weak argument on its own.

## History: primary documents, all open

These are not papers, and pieces built on them still meet the five obligations, with the document in
place of the paper. All four were reachable on August 27, 2026.

- **Mapping Inequality**, University of Richmond. **There is a Greenville, SC HOLC map**:
  `https://dsl.richmond.edu/panorama/redlining/map/SC/Greenville`. The area descriptions are
  scanned and quotable.
- **FRASER**, the St. Louis Fed's archive, which holds the **FHA Underwriting Manual**:
  `https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/underwriting-manual-1024`. The document that wrote the rules
  the maps enforced.
- **CourtListener** for case law. *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty* (1926) is where American
  zoning became constitutional, and every setback line in Greenville County descends from it.
- **FRED** for the current Greenville and national series, when a piece grounds itself in a figure.

Fair housing applies in full to anything written from the first two. The subject is what
institutions did and what the record shows they did; the piece never characterizes who lives
anywhere now.

## How this file grows

The scout adds every good paper it meets, with the same fields: authors, year, title, venue,
citations, access status, and one line on what the trick is. **It never adds a paper it has not
seen in an index**, and it never invents an NBER number: an NBER working paper is only cited by
number when the DOI began `10.3386` or the PDF actually fetched.

Alex seeds this file too, and his entries outrank harvested ones. A paper he is curious about is
worth more than a paper with more citations, because he is the first reader.

Harvesting queries that worked, for reuse:

```
https://api.openalex.org/works?filter=title_and_abstract.search:<terms>,is_oa:true
  &sort=cited_by_count:desc&per-page=25&mailto=<email>
```

Use `title_and_abstract.search`. The bare `default.search` filter is too loose and returned
pandemic and asset-pricing papers for a housing query.
