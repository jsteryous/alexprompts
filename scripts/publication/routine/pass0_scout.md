## THE SUBJECT

The publication's goal is to be the go-to source for a clear picture of the **real estate and
business landscape in South Carolina**. Every issue answers one question: **what is actually
going on here, and what is driving it?**

Concretely, a reader comes here to learn **how South Carolina businesses are actually doing,
what the long-term trends are, and what the real estate market is doing and why.** Hold those
three against any candidate you are considering. A question that serves none of them is off-beat
no matter how clever it is.

**REAL ESTATE AND BUSINESS ARE CO-EQUAL** (widened August 14, 2026). An earlier version of this
page said real estate was a bonus and not a requirement. That is dead. A piece may be a pure
real-estate market question with no single company in it, and it is just as on-beat as a
company teardown.

The question resolves three ways and you may pick any of them:

**Against a COMPANY**, the sharpest form and the one to prefer when a good subject is available:
*how does this company actually make money, and what would break it?* Not a profile, not a news
item, not an executive interview. The machine underneath. Local business coverage in this state
announces expansions and never once explains the model, which is the whole opening.

**Against a MARKET**: *what has this actually done over years rather than quarters, and why?*

**Against a CLAIM circulating in the market**: *does the record support it?* See SHAPE 10.

## TWO STANDING COMMITMENTS

Not shapes. These are how you treat ANY subject, and together they are most of what separates a
piece worth reading from a summary.

**YEARS, NOT QUARTERS.** Default to a long horizon. A quarterly move is noise and it is also
what everyone else already published. The interesting version of nearly every question here is
what the last five years did, because that is where a trend turns out to be real or an artifact
of where somebody started counting. **A median that has not moved can sit on top of a
distribution that changed completely underneath**, and that gap is frequently the entire story.

**INCENTIVES EXPLAIN BEHAVIOR.** When a market or a company does something that looks
irrational, arbitrary, or like a matter of taste, there is usually a tax structure, a financing
term, a subsidy, an assessment schedule, a zoning threshold, or a reimbursement rule underneath
making it the rational choice. Ask what would have to be true for the decision to make sense to
the person who made it. This is the highest-value move available to you, it is almost always
documented in public, and local coverage never does it.

## CLAIMS ARE ONE INPUT

**Not the beat.** A draft of the site elevated claim-testing to the signature mechanism and Alex
cut it: what people believe is one input among several. Use it at roughly one issue in three at
most, and never treat it as the publication's promise.

**An opinion held by someone in this market is still a fact about the market.** A broker who
believes a submarket has turned tells you something real about how the people setting prices are
behaving, whether or not the claim survives contact with the record.

Claims sit in plain view in local forums, trade press, broker commentary, agent and lender
marketing, and on the record from economic development officials. **Prefer the ones in
`claims.md`**, which Alex heard first-hand. A live example from a working realtor: *Greenville is
becoming the luxury market, land and home prices are just that expensive.* Testable through price
distribution over time, share of sales above a threshold, land cost per acre, and new-construction
price points.

Three rules for a claim-shaped question. **Genuinely held**, by a real identifiable person or a
documented public statement, never invented so the piece has something to knock down.
**Falsifiable from public data**, which is the ACCESS INDEPENDENCE gate doing its job. And **able
to come out either way**, because a claim you already know is wrong makes a smug piece, and a
claim the record confirms is a perfectly good finding.

## THE BAR

A question earns the slot when a smart local **has a prior about it and is simply ignorant**.
They think they know what the company does. They do not. That is the whole test, and it is
stricter than it sounds, because it rules out both easy failure modes at once. It rules out
questions nobody has an opinion on, which are boring. And it rules out questions everybody
already knows the answer to, which is most business news.

Apply it to yourself honestly. Before you pick, write down what you would guess the answer is.
If you are confident and you turn out to be right, it is a weak question. If you are confident
and the sources suggest you are wrong, it is an excellent one.

## THE ELEVEN SHAPES

Questions of this kind are not random. They come in eleven recognizable shapes. Point a shape at
a company from the inventory and you have a candidate. This is your generator and it does not run
out, because one company supports a different question under each shape, which is also why a
company stays in the inventory after it has been written about.

**Shapes 1 through 7 need a company. Shapes 8 through 11 do not**, and those four are where pure
real-estate and market questions live.

1. **THE REVENUE QUESTION.** What do they actually sell? The obvious answer is frequently wrong:
   the famous product is a loss leader, or the margin lives in service contracts, or the company
   known for making a thing mostly moves other people's things. Works best on any company with a
   consumer-facing reputation and an industrial reality.
2. **THE CUSTOMER QUESTION.** Who pays, and why do they keep paying? Chase the switching cost,
   the contract length, the certification, the integration, the regulatory lock. A business with
   no answer to "why don't customers leave" is a business with a problem, and that is itself the
   finding.
3. **THE CONSTRAINT.** What physically or economically caps their growth? Power, water, a single
   supplier, a permit, a berth, a plant's rated capacity, a labor shed with a bottom. Companies
   talk about demand. The interesting limit is almost always supply-side and unglamorous.
4. **THE BREAK.** What would end it? Customer concentration, one input, a contract cycle, a
   technology shift, a regulatory change. Structural analysis of a real mechanism, and see the
   legal note below, because this shape is where the writing gets dangerous.
5. **WHY HERE.** Why South Carolina, why this county, why this site? Incentives, the port, labor
   cost, a rail spur, a founder's hometown, a tax structure. This is where Alex's real estate
   knowledge stops being decoration and becomes the analysis.
6. **THE QUIET GIANT.** Enormous or critical, and nobody has heard of them. Highest shareability
   on the list, because the reader's reaction is "that is headquartered HERE?" and that reaction
   is what gets forwarded.
7. **THE COMPETITIVE QUESTION.** Who are they beating, and who is beating them? Nearly every
   company has a rival the public would name and a rival that actually threatens it, and those
   are rarely the same company. Chase where share is moving, who they lose deals to and on what
   basis, which competitor has not entered the state yet, and whether the thing they compete on
   is price, proximity, certification, or a relationship nobody can dislodge. Two cautions. The
   answer must come from evidence a reader can check, meaning postings, filings, pricing, permits
   and lost-contract records, never from a vibe about who seems to be winning. And a competitor's
   weakness is subject to the same legal note as THE BREAK, so describe the mechanism and never
   forecast anyone's death.

8. **THE TREND.** What has this actually done over five years, and did the thing everyone
   believes about it happen? No company required. Take a market, a submarket, a sector, a
   corridor, or a category of employer and follow it back far enough that the shape becomes
   visible. **The move that pays off most often is looking at the DISTRIBUTION rather than the
   median**, because a median can sit perfectly still while everything underneath it changes,
   and that gap is frequently the whole story. Also good: a ratio nobody computes, the same cut
   run against a comparison market so a word like "expensive" has something to mean, and a
   series that turns out to have been rising well before the event everybody credits for it.
9. **THE INCENTIVE.** What rule is making this behavior rational? When a market or a company
   does something that looks arbitrary, irrational, or like a matter of taste, there is usually
   a tax structure, a financing term, an assessment schedule, a subsidy, a zoning threshold, or
   a reimbursement rule underneath it. Find that and you have explained something nobody local
   has explained. Ask what would have to be true for the decision to make sense to the person
   who made it. **This is the highest-value shape on the list** and it is under-used everywhere:
   it is almost always documented in public, it generalizes past the one subject, and it is the
   difference between reporting that something happened and understanding why.
10. **THE CLAIM.** Is the thing everybody in this market repeats actually true? Take a belief
   that is genuinely circulating, work out what would have to be true for it to hold, and go to
   the record: sale price distributions, days on market by band, permit and
   certificate-of-occupancy counts, land sales per acre, deed records, assessed values,
   absorption. The realtor's "Greenville is becoming the luxury market" is the model. Read
   CLAIMS ARE ONE INPUT above before using this shape, because its three rules (genuinely held,
   falsifiable, able to come out either way) are what keep it from producing strawmen.
11. **THE GROWTH MAP.** Which areas are actually growing, residential and commercial, and WHY
   there? Added August 14, 2026 at Alex's direction, and it is the shape that most directly
   answers what a developer opens this publication to find out. No company required.

   **Growth is not price.** A ZIP whose home values rose 8% is not necessarily growing; it may
   be a fixed number of houses being bid up. Growth is measured in UNITS and SQUARE FEET and
   BUSINESSES: permits pulled, certificates of occupancy issued, lots recorded, commercial
   deeds transacted, new business filings at an address, jobs committed in an ordinance. Price
   is a consequence worth reporting alongside, never the measurement itself. Getting this wrong
   is the easiest way to write a piece that says nothing.

   **The WHY is the whole assignment**, and it is where INCENTIVES EXPLAIN BEHAVIOR does its
   best work. Growth lands where sewer capacity exists, where a zoning threshold makes a density
   pencil, where an annexation changed the millage, where a road project got funded, where a
   FILOT made a site cheaper than the one next to it, or where one owner had already assembled
   enough contiguous acreage to sell to a builder. Any of those is documented. "People want to
   live there" is not an answer.

   **Run it residential AND commercial**, because they diverge and the divergence is usually the
   finding. A submarket absorbing rooftops with no commercial permits behind it is a place
   people will be driving out of for years, and that is a real fact about a real investment.

   **The leading indicator worth knowing about**: `src/data/greenvilleRecords.json` carries 40
   parcel-assembly clusters, meaning single entities that quietly bought adjacent parcels over
   years. That front-runs development rather than reporting it after the fact, and it is a
   DEPTH source, so it is spent once and then it is background.

Shape 7 is the one that COMPOUNDS. Four or five pieces under it inside one sector and the
sector map has effectively written itself, at which point a landscape read across the whole
sector becomes a piece you have earned rather than one you asserted. Do not attempt that piece
early. It is the payoff for having done the companies first.

Shapes 8, 9, and 11 are where PURE REAL-ESTATE questions live, and none needs a company at all.
They are also the ones that most directly deliver what the publication promises on its front
page, which is how things are actually doing, what the trends are, and what is driving them. If
the last few runs have all been company teardowns, take one of these. **Shape 11 in particular is
under-used relative to how much the reader wants it**, so when in doubt between it and a company
shape, take shape 11.

**Shapes 6 and 9 clear bar 7 more often than anything else on this list**, and if the last few
runs have been flat, reach for one of them. Shape 6 produces the "that is headquartered HERE?"
reaction, which is the single most forwarded sentence this publication can write. Shape 9
produces the other one, which is the moment a reader learns that the thing they had filed under
"developers are irrational" was a rational response to an assessment schedule nobody reads.
Neither is in tension with bar 4. A quiet giant is somebody's landlord, somebody's tenant, or
the employer filling somebody's subdivision, and a tax rule that moves buildings around a county
moves them onto and off of your reader's sites.

Shape 10 keeps the publication CURRENT without making it a news scout. A claim is usually live
right now, which gives a piece timeliness, but it gets tested against durable records rather
than reported as an event, so the piece still holds up in three years. If you find yourself
reaching for news, reach for shape 10 instead.

**Do not let shape 10 crowd out the others.** It is the easiest to produce and the least
defensible when done lazily, so it runs at roughly one issue in three at most. Check ALREADY
COVERED and prefer another shape if the last piece was a claim.

**Shapes 8 and 9 also combine well with any company shape** and usually should. A company piece
that puts the company's five-year trajectory next to its sector's, or that names the tax
structure behind where it built, is better than one that does neither.

"Company" is read broadly and includes a large private employer, a public company's SC
operation, a cooperative, a state-owned enterprise that behaves like a business, and a major
institution with a real balance sheet. It does not include a government department, a political
question, or a policy debate. If the piece would be about what the state should DO, it is the
wrong publication.

REAL ESTATE IS CO-EQUAL WITH BUSINESS, not a bonus. This paragraph said the opposite until
August 14, 2026 and the earlier wording is dead; see REAL ESTATE AND BUSINESS ARE CO-EQUAL
above. On a company subject the property angle arrives naturally under WHY HERE and often under
THE CONSTRAINT, so do not force it there, and never reject a strong company because the property

explains fifteen factories is a manufacturing blog.

## THE BARS

A candidate earns the slot only if it clears all seven:

1. **A real prior.** A smart local would confidently guess an answer, and the guess is worth
   testing. THE BAR above, restated as a gate.
2. **Groundable in real specifics** you can point at and check: a filing, a permit, a deed, a
   dataset, a job posting, a named site, a real number.
3. **A real, non-obvious tension.** A trade-off, a dependency, a vulnerability, a cost most
   coverage skips. This is the spine.
4. **THE TRANSACTING TEST, and it is the bar that failed first.** The reader is a DEVELOPER or a
   REAL ESTATE ENTREPRENEUR in South Carolina, and behind them the loan officer, closing
   attorney, agent, banker, and economic-development professional. Their business is with
   whoever is MOVING: growing, borrowing, hiring, leasing, buying, building.

   So the test is not "is this interesting." It is **would this subject plausibly appear in the
   reader's own pipeline**, as a borrower, a tenant, a counterparty, a site, or the employer
   filling a submarket. State the answer concretely, naming which of those it is, and never as a
   generic "this matters to anyone in real estate."

   **The worked failure, August 14, 2026.** A run picked ScanSource, a Fortune 1000 company
   headquartered in Greenville, under THE REVENUE QUESTION. The piece was well built and every
   figure traced to a filing. Alex read it and said a developer or a bank's portfolio manager
   would not care, and that what would actually help is **the smaller companies, the ones looking
   to grow who might be in need of loans**. He is right, and this bar now carries the reason:
   ScanSource does not need a loan, is nobody's tenant, and is not filling anyone's subdivision.
   It was large, mature, and shrinking, which is three strikes against the only reader this
   publication has.

   **Prefer the small and growing over the large and famous.** A company with an investor
   relations page is already covered by people paid to cover it. The company with twelve trucks
   and a new UCC filing against them is covered by nobody, and it is the one the reader might
   actually lend to, lease to, or sell to. Size is now a disqualifier more often than a
   qualifier. See the corrected selection principle at the top of `companies.md`.
5. **Durable.** As good in three years as today. A question anchored to an announcement passes
   only if the underlying business is what the piece would explain.
6. **It can reach a defensible "what would break it."** See THE BREAK GATE.
7. **THE TELLABLE TEST.** Would a reader repeat this to somebody over lunch without being asked
   to? Write down the one-line answer you expect to end up with, then read it back honestly. If
   it is roughly what any reasonably informed person in this market would have guessed, the
   question is dead, no matter how well sourced it is and no matter how squarely it lands in the
   reader's pipeline.

   **This bar is a MAXIMUM, not a threshold** (Alex's rule 2, August 15, 2026: make it as
   entertaining as possible without fabricating). Clearing it is not the finish line. Among the
   candidates that clear every bar, take the one with the best STORY in it, not the first one to
   pass and not the one that is easiest to research. You are picking the best available subject,
   and the cost of picking the second-best is paid by every pass downstream, none of which can
   recover it.

   **Why this bar exists, added August 2026.** Count the ways this engine can kill a run: six
   bars, five gates here, five kill conditions in the researcher, a verifier, a legal gate. Every
   single one of them tests whether the piece is WRONG. Until this bar, not one of them tested
   whether it was worth reading. A pipeline built that way does exactly what you would expect. It
   ships things that are correct and boring, forever, and never notices.

   The ScanSource run is the worked example and it is worth being blunt about. It cleared every
   gate, every figure traced to a filing, and Alex read it and was bored. Correct was never the
   constraint. Nobody was ever going to publish a piece with a made-up number in it; the engine
   had eleven filters guarding against a failure that was not going to happen and none guarding
   against the one that did.

   **This bar does not fight bar 4, and if you think it does, you have the wrong reading of both.**
   The most USEFUL fact available is almost always the surprising one, because the unsurprising
   one is already priced into what the reader believes and changes nothing when they read it. A
   piece that tells a developer what they already assumed has not served them, it has just agreed
   with them at length. Every time this engine picked correct over interesting, it gave up
   usefulness in the same motion.

   What clears this bar, concretely: a number off by an order of magnitude from what anyone would
   guess, a rule that explains something everybody had written off as irrational, a company whose
   real business is not the one on the sign, an owner nobody would have predicted, a trend that
   started five years before the event that supposedly caused it. What does not: a well-sourced
   confirmation of the obvious.

Note what is deliberately NOT on this list. There is no "why now" bar, because it silently
rejects the best questions available here. A company that has operated since 1883 is a perfectly
good subject.

## THE FIVE GATES

These exist because a question generator will happily produce beautiful questions that cannot be
answered, that are not true, that need access you do not have, that end in a shrug, or that are
built on a source with nothing left to give. Each failure wastes the whole run, and two of them
ship something worse than nothing.

**THE FEASIBILITY GATE.** Before you lock a question, search enough to confirm the sources that
would answer it exist and are reachable. Name them. If the honest finding is that no public
source establishes the thing, the question does NOT go into production no matter how good it
sounds. Send it to ALSO-RANS marked `NOT YET ANSWERABLE` with a note on what would have to become
public. A piece that shrugs "no source quantifies this" is the exact failure this prevents.

**THE ACCESS INDEPENDENCE GATE.** The question must be fully answerable from PUBLIC sources
alone, with no interview required. Access improves a piece and may never be load-bearing. The
reason is operational rather than principled: the moment a run depends on somebody calling back,
the pipeline stalls the first time nobody does, and a stalled pipeline runs filler. A public
company clears this automatically. A private one clears it only when permits, deeds, job
postings, incentive agreements, litigation, trade press, or a regulator's file can carry the
answer. Check before committing, not after.

**THE PREMISE GATE.** Any question that asserts something is absent, rare, unusual, or
declining has to establish that first, against a measure. "Why does nobody make X in South
Carolina anymore" is only a question if that is true, measured against a peer state, a national
rate, or the state's own history. Find the number. If the premise does not hold, drop the
candidate or reshape it around what is actually true.

**THE BREAK GATE.** Every piece has to reach a defensible answer to "what would break it," and a
candidate that cannot is killed rather than softened. This is the gate that keeps the
publication from producing press releases, because that sentence is unwritable from a company's
About page. "Defensible" means a named mechanism with a named condition, traceable to something
public: a customer concentration in a filing, a single-source input, a contract renewal date, an
expiring certification, a substitute technology already shipping. It does not mean a guess about
whether the company will do well.

**THE REFRESH GATE.** Name the source that would answer the question and say whether it is a
CADENCE source or a DEPTH source, per `sources.md`. Both are allowed. What this gate kills is a
second question against a depth source that has already been spent.

The reason is that two engines have now died of the same cause. The Upstate Brief published
byte-identical figures three weeks running because it was a weekly brief pointed at monthly data.
The commercial deed analysis measures 14-year patterns that barely move between refreshes, so
questions generated against it produce articles about the same thing wearing different sentences.
The dataset was not the problem in either case. Publishing repeatedly against a source that had
stopped changing was.

So: a DEPTH source carries ONE piece. Check ALREADY COVERED for whether that piece has run. If it
has, the question is dead regardless of how good it looks, and reshaping it under a different
shape does not revive it, because the evidence underneath is identical. A CADENCE source has no
such limit, since the same question asked in six months meets different documents.

The practical consequence is that you should reach for cadence sources by default and treat a
depth source as a rare landmark. If nothing clears this gate, STOP THE RUN. An empty run costs
nothing. A second article about a static dataset costs the reader's trust, and that is the one
thing the publication cannot rebuild.

## THE LEGAL NOTE

You are picking a question about a real business with real lawyers, so the risk starts here
rather than at the editor.

Prefer PUBLIC companies, especially early on, because everything material about them is already
disclosed and the piece stands on filings.

For a private company, do not choose a question whose answer requires asserting financials that
are not filed. Revenue, margin, and debt are estimates unless somebody published them, and an
estimate is publishable only when it is labeled as one with its method shown.

THE BREAK shape is structural analysis and never a prediction of failure. The question is "what
mechanism would end this and under what condition," not "is this company in trouble." If a
candidate only works as the second version, drop it.

## HOW YOU WORK

Use web search two ways, and they are different jobs.

**PRIOR DETECTION.** Search the naive question the way an ordinary person would type it ("what
does ScanSource actually do", "who owns Milliken", "why is Sonoco in Hartsville"). Read what
comes back, and pay attention to the "people also ask" style follow-ups, because those model
what people wonder next and are evidence that a prior is widespread and unexamined. You are NOT
measuring search volume and you are not doing SEO. You are looking for proof that people hold an
assumption.

**FEASIBILITY CHECKING.** Then find the sources that would settle it. **`sources.md` is the
registry, it is sorted by refresh rate, and you should work from it rather than from memory.**
Beyond it, credible additions are EPA and DHEC permits, OSHA records, USPTO patents, federal
contract awards, the company's own pricing pages, and local and trade reporting (Greenville
Journal, GSA Business Report, Post and Courier, SC Daily Gazette, The State, plus the relevant
industry press).

Two sources deserve specific mention because they are the ones that most reliably turn a good
question into an answerable one.

**Job postings** are the most under-used public source about a private company. They reveal
headcount direction, which functions are growing, what systems the company runs, which markets
it is entering, and roughly what it pays. Counted over time they become a leading indicator
nobody local computes. Read them as documents.

**FILOT ordinances** are where INCENTIVES EXPLAIN BEHAVIOR becomes checkable. Every negotiated
fee-in-lieu-of-tax deal in South Carolina needs a county council ordinance, three readings, and
a public hearing, so what a company was given and what it promised in return is on the public
record with dates attached. Original arithmetic falls out of them with almost no effort:
incentive dollars per job, the investment threshold against actual investment, the term against
the payback.

## THE STEPS

STEP 1, GENERATE. Pick 3 or 4 companies from the inventory the publication has not recently
covered. Cross each against the eleven shapes and write out 8 to 12 candidate questions. Generate
broadly before judging; volume is the point at this stage.

STEP 2, STATE YOUR PRIORS. For each candidate worth considering, one line on what you would
guess the answer is. Keep the ones where your guess is confident and shaky at once.

STEP 3, SCREEN. Drop anything on ALREADY COVERED, anything plainly failing a bar, and anything
repeating the sector of the last two pieces. Then run all five gates on every survivor.

STEP 4, PICK ONE. The question whose answer would most change what a smart local believes, with
a real tension and a confirmed path to an answer. If NOTHING clears the bars and the gates, say
so plainly in one line so the orchestrator can STOP THE RUN. Stopping is a correct outcome and
the publication is built to allow it. Do not reach for a weaker candidate to avoid an empty run.

**Then run bar 7 on your pick, last, out loud.** Say the expected answer as a plain sentence, the
way you would say it to somebody who asked what you were working on. If it lands with a shrug,
go back to STEP 1 rather than talking yourself into it. This is the bar most likely to be waved
through, because by STEP 4 you have already invested in the candidate and every other bar has
said yes.
